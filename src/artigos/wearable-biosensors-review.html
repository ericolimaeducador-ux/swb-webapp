Wearable Biosensors in Chronic Wounds: Clinical Promise and Translational Barriers

A critical narrative review of wearable biosensors and smart dressings in chronic wounds, distinguishing analytical promise from clinically validated usefulness and defining the translational barriers that still limit routine adoption.

Updated manuscript Scientific review SWB Evidence Hub

Authors: Érico Henrique de Lima Araújo, RN, MSc Candidate; Rivelino Verdier Dantas Montenegro, PhD.

Abstract

Background: Chronic wound assessment remains dominated by episodic visual inspection and descriptive documentation. These methods are clinically necessary but do not continuously capture wound pH, temperature, oxygenation, moisture, exudate chemistry or host-microbial activity. Wearable biosensors and smart dressings have therefore emerged as a distinct technological pathway within, but not identical to, the broader digital wound care ecosystem.

Hypothesis / Aim: This critical narrative review examines the biological rationale, technology classes, translational readiness, human feasibility evidence and implementation barriers for wearable biosensors in chronic wounds. Its specific aim is to distinguish analytical promise from clinically validated usefulness and to define where the field is most ready for translation.

Methods: Literature was reviewed across PubMed/MEDLINE, Embase, CINAHL, IEEE Xplore and the Cochrane Library. Searches combined terms related to chronic wounds, wound healing, wearable biosensors, smart dressings, electrochemical sensors, colorimetric sensors, microfluidics, wound exudate, pH, temperature, oxygen, infection, telemedicine and digital wound care. Publications from January 2020 to March 2025 were prioritised, with earlier seminal human or methodological studies included when directly relevant.

Results / Findings: The evidence base supports strong biological plausibility and rapid technological progress, particularly for electrochemical, microfluidic and flexible bioelectronic platforms. However, human evidence remains limited to observational and early feasibility studies. The principal barriers to clinical adoption are wound-type heterogeneity, inconsistent wound-fluid sampling, reliance on surrogate analytical endpoints, limited comparator-controlled studies and insufficient demonstration of patient-important outcomes.

Conclusions: Wearable biosensors have moved beyond speculative engineering and now show credible translational maturity. They should currently be regarded as adjunctive decision-support technologies, not replacements for clinical assessment.

Implications for clinical practice: Clinicians should interpret biosensor outputs as adjunctive data within structured wound assessment pathways; use them, when available, to support defined decisions such as dressing-change timing or escalation review; and avoid treating analytical agreement alone as proof of clinical effectiveness.

Keywords

Chronic wounds; Digital wound care; Smart dressings; Telemedicine; Wearable biosensors; Wound exudate.

Key messages
  • Wearable biosensors offer continuous wound microenvironment monitoring that conventional episodic inspection cannot provide, but they remain an adjunct to clinical assessment.
  • This review critically synthesises biological targets, device classes, human evidence and implementation barriers using a three-stage translational readiness framework.
  • Current evidence supports analytical feasibility and early clinical use on real wounds; comparative effectiveness, indication-specific thresholds and workflow impact remain insufficiently validated.

Introduction

Chronic wounds are wounds that fail to progress through an expected healing trajectory because of prolonged inflammation, impaired perfusion, infection, repeated tissue injury and systemic comorbidity [1]. Their burden is not only biological; chronic wounds are associated with pain, immobility, reduced quality of life, repeated healthcare contact and substantial resource consumption [1,2]. Conventional wound assessment remains heavily dependent on episodic inspection, descriptive documentation and visual interpretation. Although these approaches remain indispensable, they do not fully capture dynamic changes in wound pH, temperature, oxygenation, moisture, exudate chemistry or host-microbial interactions. This limitation has stimulated interest in wearable biosensors and smart dressings capable of providing more continuous and objective monitoring [3,4].

At the same time, the broader digital wound care ecosystem has expanded rapidly. Telemedicine, wound photography, remote measurement software, app-based follow-up and machine-learning-supported classification are increasingly discussed alongside wearable biosensing. However, these interventions are not equivalent. Digital platforms that document wound size or enable remote review do not necessarily monitor biochemical changes in the wound bed. Likewise, a flexible sensor patch that measures wound biomarkers addresses a different clinical question from a teleconsultation system.

The added value of this review is a three-stage translational readiness framework for wearable wound biosensors. Stage 1 is analytical validation in controlled conditions, where a sensor demonstrates reliable measurement of a defined parameter. Stage 2 is clinical feasibility and concordance in real wound populations, where the device can be worn, generate usable data and show agreement with appropriate reference methods. Stage 3 is comparative clinical effectiveness, where sensor-guided care improves patient-important or workflow-relevant outcomes in adequately powered, indication-specific studies.

The purpose of this review is therefore to critically examine wearable biosensors in chronic wounds as a distinct technological and clinical domain. We focus on the biological rationale for continuous monitoring, the main technology classes, preclinical and translational maturity, human feasibility evidence, implementation context, and the current limitations that prevent immediate large-scale adoption.

Methods

This article is a critical narrative review. Searches were conducted in PubMed/MEDLINE, Embase, CINAHL, IEEE Xplore and the Cochrane Library. Search concepts included chronic wound, wound healing, pressure injury, diabetic foot ulcer, venous leg ulcer, wound exudate, smart dressing, wearable biosensor, flexible sensor, electrochemical sensor, colorimetric sensor, optical sensor, microfluidic, pH, temperature, oxygen, glucose, lactate, uric acid, hydrogen peroxide, infection, digital wound care, telemedicine and remote wound monitoring.

Publications from January 2020 to March 2025 were prioritised because this period captures the recent acceleration in flexible electronics, microfluidics and digital wound care. Earlier seminal studies were retained where they provided human feasibility evidence, methodological context or foundational digital wound care guidance.

Studies were selected iteratively rather than through formal PRISMA screening. Priority was given to evidence that addressed at least one of the following: biological plausibility of a wound biomarker; analytical performance of a wearable or dressing-integrated sensor; use on human wounds; distinction between analytical validation and clinical outcomes; implementation of digital wound care; or regulatory evaluation of device-software systems.

Critical appraisal was pragmatic and narrative. Each source was assessed for study design, sample size, wound population definition, comparator use, sampling method, analytical reference standard, follow-up duration, clinical endpoint relevance and risk of conflating analytical agreement with clinical effectiveness.

Results

Biological rationale for monitoring

The biological rationale for wearable wound monitoring is strong. Chronic wounds are characterised by dysregulated inflammation, altered perfusion, protease imbalance, variable microbial burden and unstable microenvironmental conditions [1]. Reviews of wound biosensing consistently identify pH, temperature, oxygen, moisture and exudate-derived biomarkers as priority targets because they are mechanistically linked to infection risk, tissue viability, inflammatory activity and delayed repair [3,4].

However, biological plausibility is not the same as clinical actionability. Wound fluid collection remains methodologically heterogeneous, and proteomic or inflammatory biomarker discovery is limited by inconsistent sampling, small samples and short observation periods [12]. Recent translational work suggests that integrated immunomarker ratios may be more informative than isolated mediators, but these findings require replication and external validation before they can support routine decision thresholds [13].

Technology classes

Current platforms span colorimetric and optical sensors, electrochemical sensors, microfluidic systems, flexible bioelectronics, multiplexed arrays and theranostic systems [3,4]. Colorimetric systems are attractive for pH sensing because of simplicity and low cost, but they are vulnerable to environmental variation and often require optical aids or smartphone quantification to reduce subjectivity. Electrochemical systems are the most quantitatively mature class, offering high sensitivity, compatibility with flexible substrates and growing capacity for wireless integration.

Microfluidic architecture is not merely an engineering refinement. In exudative wounds, controlled collection, refreshing and analyte transport are prerequisites for reliable sensing. Multiplexed and flexible bioelectronic systems increasingly aim to reflect the multifactorial biology of chronic wounds rather than relying on a single marker, but this complexity creates additional validation and regulatory burdens.

Preclinical and translational evidence

The current literature shows convincing progress along the translational pathway, but maturity remains uneven. A scoping review of smart dressings for diagnosing infection concluded that the field is still dominated by proof-of-concept and animal or ex vivo studies, with limited late-stage clinical validation [9]. More recent studies demonstrate meaningful translational gains. The iSAFE platform described a battery-free, skin-adhesive, stretchable wound bandage with sensing and adaptive therapeutic capability, including a small clinical feasibility component [10]. The iCares platform integrated wound exudate management with microfluidics and multiplex sensing of NO, H2O2, O2, pH and temperature [11].

These platforms show that the field is no longer restricted to laboratory prototypes. Yet they remain primarily Stage 1 to Stage 2 technologies in the translational readiness framework. They demonstrate analytical sophistication and early feasibility, but do not yet establish comparative effectiveness, improved healing, earlier infection treatment or reduced service burden compared with standard wound assessment.

Human clinical evidence

Direct human evidence remains limited but real. The older WoundSense observational study showed that 44.9% of dressing changes in monitored wounds occurred when moisture remained in the optimal zone, suggesting that many dressing changes may be premature [8]. This finding supports operational usefulness, but not improved healing or reduced infection.

More recent human evidence comes from early feasibility studies. In iSAFE, a small clinical study involving patients with acute and chronic wounds showed that the device could be applied on real wounds and that its sensors for glucose, pH and temperature achieved acceptable agreement with reference instruments [10]. In iCares, data from 20 patients with chronic lower limb wounds and two perioperative patients were used together with machine learning for classification and healing-time prediction [11].

Digital wound care as adjacent evidence

The wider digital literature provides implementation context rather than direct proof for biosensors. Digital wound care can improve process efficiency and access, but it should not be treated as surrogate evidence for the effectiveness of biochemical or bioelectronic wearables. Instead, it defines the implementation environment into which biosensors must fit: remote review, structured documentation, image capture, workflow triage, patient education and data governance.

Implementation and regulatory context

Economic and implementation arguments are promising but incomplete. Translation into practice requires more than technical performance. Barriers repeatedly identified in the literature include cost, biofouling, signal drift, energy supply, patient adherence, workflow integration, data governance and regulatory classification [3,4]. The IMDRF clinical evaluation framework is particularly relevant to biosensor systems that combine hardware, software and analytics because it distinguishes clinical association, analytical validation and clinical validation as separate but necessary steps [17].

Discussion

Integrative synthesis

This review supports a clear conclusion: wearable biosensors in chronic wound care have entered a phase of credible translational maturity, but not yet a phase of mature implementation. The biological rationale for monitoring wound microenvironmental change is strong, and technology development has moved beyond simple concept devices toward flexible, multiplexed and, in some cases, therapeutic systems [3,4,10,11]. However, biomarker plausibility, sensor accuracy and clinical usefulness are sequential requirements, not interchangeable achievements.

The three-stage translational readiness framework helps locate the field. Many platforms have achieved Stage 1 analytical validation. A smaller number, including WoundSense, iSAFE and iCares, have reached Stage 2 clinical feasibility or concordance in real wound contexts [8,10,11]. Stage 3 comparative clinical effectiveness remains largely unproven.

Why the translational gap persists

Three methodological weaknesses explain why the gap persists. First, wound-type stratification is inadequate. Second, many studies rely on surrogate analytical endpoints such as signal agreement, biomarker detection or prediction accuracy. Third, sampling and threshold methods remain inconsistent. Without standardised exudate collection, calibration procedures and clinically meaningful thresholds, it is difficult to compare devices or define actionable trigger points across settings [12,13].

Clinical decision pathways: potential impact scenarios

Infection surveillance is the most clinically compelling but also the most demanding use case. A sustained pattern of temperature rise, alkaline pH shift, oxygen change or inflammatory biomarker alteration could trigger earlier clinical review before overt deterioration. Dressing-change optimisation is a more immediately testable use case. Healing trajectory monitoring is a third plausible pathway, especially when integrated biomarker profiles, oxygen trends, temperature patterns and wound fluid signals support triage in wound clinics.

Clinical utility by platform class

Colorimetric and optical systems are most plausible as low-cost adjuncts for dressing-change support. Electrochemical and microfluidic systems are better positioned for infection surveillance and healing-trajectory monitoring because they can provide quantitative and multiplexed signals. Flexible bioelectronic systems may be useful where continuous temperature or impedance monitoring is integrated into a broader digital care pathway. Theranostic systems are the most ambitious class and require the strongest validation.

Implementation and regulatory implications

Wearable biosensors are unlikely to succeed as stand-alone gadgets. Their future lies within hybrid care pathways that combine wound imaging, remote review, structured documentation and biosensor outputs. Implementation studies should evaluate training, patient adherence, data burden, alarm fatigue, interoperability and responsibility for acting on sensor signals.

Conclusion

Wearable biosensors represent one of the most promising emerging areas in chronic wound care. The field now includes biologically plausible targets, increasingly sophisticated technology platforms and early human studies demonstrating feasibility and analytical promise. However, the present evidence base remains insufficient to support claims of generalised clinical superiority or routine large-scale use. At this stage, wearable biosensors should be understood as adjunctive technologies with substantial future potential, not as established replacements for conventional assessment.

Implications for clinical practice

  • Use wearable biosensor outputs as adjunctive information within structured wound assessment; do not use them as stand-alone substitutes for clinical judgement, wound history or standard examination.
  • Where biosensors are available, link their output to a predefined decision pathway, such as escalation review for suspected infection, dressing-change timing, or triage of wounds that are not following an expected healing trajectory.
  • Do not interpret analytical agreement with reference instruments as proof of clinical effectiveness. Clinical adoption should require evidence that sensor-guided care improves patient outcomes, workflow, safety or cost in the relevant wound population.

Further research

  • Future studies should evaluate wearable biosensors in clearly defined wound populations, with stratification by wound type and clinically meaningful endpoints rather than proof-of-concept performance alone.
  • Multicentre validation is needed to determine whether biomarker thresholds, algorithms and workflow effects are reproducible across settings, wound types and patient groups.
  • Prospective studies should incorporate health-economic, usability, regulatory and data-governance outcomes from the outset to establish real-world value.

Wearable Biosensors and Digital Wound Monitoring in Chronic Wounds

A critical review of the emerging landscape of wearable biosensor technology applied to chronic wound monitoring, evaluating current capabilities, translational gaps, and the potential role of digital wound management in the modern wound care ecosystem.

📅 April 2026 ⏱ 18 min read 🔬 SWB Evidence Hub

Introduction

Chronic wounds — defined as wounds that fail to progress through the normal phases of healing within an expected timeframe — represent a major and growing burden on healthcare systems globally. Pressure injuries, diabetic foot ulcers, venous leg ulcers, and surgical site infections collectively affect tens of millions of patients annually, with associated costs exceeding $30 billion per year in the United States alone.

Traditional wound assessment relies on periodic visual inspection, manual measurement, and clinical judgment — a model that is inherently discontinuous, subjective, and poorly adapted to the dynamic nature of wound biology. The rise of wearable biosensor technology offers the prospect of continuous, objective, and data-rich wound monitoring, potentially transforming how chronic wounds are assessed and managed.

This review critically evaluates the current state of wearable biosensor technology in wound care, identifies translational challenges, and contextualizes their potential role alongside physical protection strategies such as anatomical barrier devices.

Integration with smart dressings

The most clinically relevant implementation of wound biosensors is their integration into smart dressings — wound contact materials that incorporate sensing elements, data transmission components, and optionally, therapeutic drug delivery capabilities. Several research groups and early-stage companies have developed prototype smart dressings that can transmit wound status data wirelessly to clinical platforms.

Emerging Technology

Smart dressings with embedded biosensors can transmit continuous wound status data — pH, temperature, moisture, biomarker levels — to clinical platforms, enabling remote monitoring and earlier intervention. Most remain in prototype or early clinical trial phases as of 2026.

Evidence Landscape

Preclinical and in vitro evidence

The preclinical evidence base for wound biosensors is robust. Multiple groups have demonstrated accurate ex vivo and in vitro detection of infection-associated pH shifts, elevated matrix metalloproteinase (MMP) activity, and inflammatory cytokine profiles using electrochemical sensor arrays integrated into dressing materials.

Animal model studies have confirmed that continuous temperature and pH monitoring can detect wound infection 24–48 hours earlier than clinical signs become apparent — a window with significant implications for antibiotic stewardship and intervention timing.

Clinical evidence: current state

Clinical translation remains in early stages. As of early 2026, the published clinical literature on wearable wound biosensors consists primarily of:

  • Proof-of-concept studies in small patient cohorts (n = 5–30)
  • Feasibility studies demonstrating sensor accuracy in clinical wound environments
  • Single-center pilot trials evaluating clinical workflow integration

Large randomized controlled trials demonstrating improved clinical outcomes attributable to biosensor-guided wound management are absent. This gap between technological promise and clinical evidence is the central challenge facing the field.

Translational Challenges

Technical barriers

Several technical challenges have slowed clinical translation. Wound exudate — the very medium biosensors are designed to analyze — is chemically complex and variable, creating signal interference that reduces sensor accuracy in real-world conditions. The adhesive and mechanical properties required for sensor-dressing integration often conflict with optimal wound interface characteristics. Battery life and wireless transmission range in clinical environments remain limiting factors for continuous monitoring applications.

Regulatory and commercial barriers

Smart wound dressings incorporating active sensing and data transmission components fall into higher regulatory risk classes in most jurisdictions, requiring more extensive clinical evidence for approval than passive dressings. This raises development costs and extends time-to-market significantly.

"The clinical translation of wound biosensors is not primarily a technology problem — it is a regulatory, economic, and implementation challenge. The sensors work. Making them clinically viable at scale requires a different kind of innovation."

Contextualizing Biosensors Within the Wound Care Ecosystem

It is important to position wearable biosensor technology not as a replacement for physical wound care interventions, but as a complementary layer within a broader wound management ecosystem. The most effective wound care strategies will likely combine:

  • Physical protection: anatomical barrier devices, appropriate dressing selection, pressure offloading
  • Continuous monitoring: biosensor-enabled early detection of microclimate deterioration, infection, and healing trajectory
  • Digital integration: clinical decision support systems informed by biosensor data streams
  • Responsive intervention: protocol-driven responses to biosensor alerts, reducing time-to-treatment

In the specific context of sacral wounds in incontinent patients, biosensor technology faces an additional challenge: the very contamination events that drive wound deterioration also compromise sensor function. Fecal matter introduces chemical noise, physical occlusion, and microbial interference that can render sensor readings unreliable. This underscores the complementary role of upstream physical barriers — such as anatomical protective devices — in preserving both wound integrity and sensor functionality.

Future Directions

The near-term future of wound biosensors is likely to be shaped by advances in three areas: material science (flexible, biocompatible sensing substrates), data infrastructure (wound-specific clinical AI platforms), and reimbursement frameworks that recognize the value of continuous monitoring in high-acuity wound populations.

Artificial intelligence integration represents a particularly promising direction. Machine learning models trained on continuous multi-parameter wound data streams could potentially predict healing trajectories, flag deterioration before clinical signs emerge, and personalize intervention timing in ways that static assessment cannot achieve.

Conclusion

Wearable biosensors and digital wound monitoring represent a genuinely transformative direction for chronic wound care, with the potential to shift management from episodic and reactive to continuous and proactive. The preclinical evidence is compelling, and early clinical feasibility data is encouraging.

However, the clinical evidence base remains immature, and significant technical, regulatory, and implementation barriers must be overcome before biosensor-guided wound management becomes standard of care. In the interim, physical protection strategies — including anatomical barrier devices for contamination prevention — remain the evidence-supported foundation of sacral wound management.

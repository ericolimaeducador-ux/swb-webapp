Layered Diapering in Bedbound Adults: A Practice-Focused Integrative Review

A practice-focused integrative review examining layered diapering in bedbound adults as a potential care-related risk through evidence from continence care, moisture-associated skin damage, incontinence-associated dermatitis, skin microclimate and pressure injury prevention.

Key messages

Overview: Layered diapering in bedbound adults is poorly studied as a direct research object, but it appears in practice as an informal response to leakage, high incontinence burden and workflow pressure.

Aim: This review examines whether layered diapering can be understood as a potential care-related risk by synthesising evidence from continence care, absorbent product use, MASD, IAD, skin microclimate and pressure injury prevention.

Findings: Direct comparative evidence is limited; however, converging evidence supports concern that layered diapering may intensify occlusion, heat retention, moisture exposure, delayed recognition of saturation and reduced skin inspection.

Abstract

Background: Adult diapers and absorbent products are commonly used in bedbound adults with urinary or faecal incontinence. In practice, two or more products may be layered to prevent leakage, protect bedding or reduce change frequency. This remains under-investigated as a discrete care behaviour.

Hypothesis/Aim: This review examined whether layered diapering in bedbound adults can be interpreted as a potential care-related risk by integrating evidence from continence care, absorbent product use, IAD, MASD, skin microclimate and pressure injury prevention.

Methods: A practice-focused integrative review with a systematised narrative approach was conducted. Eligible sources included guidelines, consensus documents, systematic reviews, health technology assessments, observational studies and relevant grey literature.

Results/Findings: Direct evidence on layered diapering is scarce. Nevertheless, the literature identifies prolonged exposure to urine and faeces, moisture, altered pH, occlusion, heat, friction and delayed skin assessment as clinically relevant contributors to skin damage.

Conclusions: Layered diapering should not be regarded as a neutral containment strategy when used routinely or to extend care intervals. It should be considered potentially inappropriate unless clinically justified, monitored and documented.

Implications for clinical practice: Care protocols should discourage routine diaper layering and prioritise timely changes, skin inspection, moisture control and continence reassessment.

Introduction

Adult diapers and absorbent products are widely used in hospitals, long-term care facilities and home care settings for adults with urinary or faecal incontinence, reduced mobility, cognitive impairment or dependence on others for hygiene. In bedbound patients, absorbent products can be clinically necessary to contain excreta, protect bedding and support care delivery. However, these products should function as part of a structured continence and skin-care plan, not as substitutes for active hygiene, timely changes and regular skin assessment.

A recurrent but under-specified practice is the use of layered diapers, also described as double diapering, diaper layering or the use of multiple absorbent products. In routine care, this may be adopted to reduce leakage, contain high-volume incontinence, protect linen, facilitate night care or manage workload during periods of limited staffing. Additional layers may increase occlusion, heat retention, product bulk and difficulty detecting saturation.

The scientific challenge is that layered diapering has rarely been studied as an intervention or exposure in its own right. The lack of direct trials, however, does not imply absence of risk. The practice can be examined through established evidence on MASD, IAD, absorbent product use, skin microclimate and pressure injury prevention.

Skin microclimate is central to this problem. Temperature, humidity and airflow at the skin-support surface interface influence tissue tolerance and may alter the threshold at which pressure, shear and friction become injurious [5]. A diaper already creates a partially occlusive environment. Layering diapers may plausibly intensify this environment, particularly in the sacral, gluteal, perineal and groin regions of immobile adults.

Aim

The aim of this review was to examine whether layered diapering in bedbound adults can be understood as a potential care-related risk by integrating evidence from adult absorbent product use, continence care, IAD, MASD, skin microclimate and pressure injury prevention.

Added value of this review

This review reframes layered diapering as a care-process issue, integrates indirect but convergent evidence from wound care, continence care and pressure injury prevention, and proposes a practical risk framework that can support nursing protocols, documentation, quality audits and future research.

Methods

Design

A practice-focused integrative review with a systematised narrative approach was conducted because direct evidence on layered diapering in bedbound adults is limited, while relevant evidence is distributed across continence care, absorbent product use, IAD, MASD, microclimate and pressure injury prevention.

Eligibility criteria

Sources were considered eligible if they addressed adult or older bedbound patients, urinary or faecal incontinence, use of diapers, pads, briefs or absorbent products, IAD, MASD, skin microclimate, pressure injury risk, nursing practice related to continence care or inappropriate use of absorbent products.

Search strategy

Searches were planned for PubMed/MEDLINE, Scopus, CINAHL, Web of Science, SciELO and LILACS. Google Scholar and Brazilian academic repositories were used as complementary sources for grey literature and national studies relevant to nursing practice.

Data extraction and synthesis

Findings were synthesised thematically into five domains: absorbent products as care technology; moisture and chemical irritation; occlusion and microclimate; delayed recognition of saturation and reduced inspection; and layered diapering as a practice-based safety concern.

Results

1. Direct evidence on layered diapering is limited

Layered diapering is rarely investigated as a discrete exposure in adult care. Most available evidence addresses broader issues: adult diapers, absorbent product performance, IAD, MASD, incontinence management and pressure injury prevention. Brazilian nursing literature supports the need for structured assessment rather than routine or convenience-based diaper use [7,8].

2. Absorbent products are care tools, not replacements for care

Absorbent products are designed to contain urinary and/or faecal leakage and support hygiene. Product selection should be matched to the patient’s continence pattern, mobility, skin condition, comfort, dignity and care context. An outer product may prevent visible leakage to bedding while the inner product remains saturated against the skin, so external containment is not equivalent to preventing moisture exposure at the skin surface.

3. Moisture and chemical irritation create a biologically plausible risk pathway

MASD is associated with repeated or prolonged exposure to moisture, including urine and stool [1,2]. IAD is a specific manifestation of this process in incontinent patients and is associated with erythema, inflammation, erosion, discomfort and a clinically relevant relationship with pressure injury risk [3,4]. Layered diapering may increase this risk through delayed recognition of saturation.

4. Occlusion, heat and microclimate are central to the risk framework

Skin microclimate refers to temperature, humidity and airflow adjacent to the skin surface. In bedbound patients, the sacral, gluteal and perineal regions are already exposed to pressure, shear, friction and occlusion. Diaper layering may add bulk and reduce ventilation, potentially intensifying local heat and humidity.

5. Layered diapering may reduce opportunities for skin inspection

Frequent skin inspection is a core component of IAD and pressure injury prevention. Layered diapering can interfere with this principle by making it more difficult to detect saturation, maceration, erythema, faecal contamination or early skin breakdown.

6. Layered diapering should be interpreted through a care-process risk model

The reviewed evidence supports a care-process model with four linked stages: baseline vulnerability from incontinence and immobility; partial occlusion from absorbent products; increased occlusion, heat, moisture retention, bulk and concealment from layering; and delayed changes or reduced inspection that may increase the probability of IAD, MASD, discomfort and pressure injury-related skin vulnerability.

Discussion

This review found limited direct evidence evaluating layered diapering in bedbound adults. However, the absence of direct comparative trials should not lead to clinical neutrality. The practice aligns with several recognised pathways of skin damage: prolonged exposure to moisture and irritants, impaired skin barrier function, increased occlusion, heat retention, friction, delayed product changes and reduced inspection.

Layered diapering should be treated as a potential care-risk behaviour when used routinely, undocumented or with the purpose of reducing change frequency. It is not simply a question of product quantity. It is a question of whether the patient’s skin remains dry, visible, inspected and protected.

Layered diapering may be understood as a signal event. Its presence should trigger a clinical question: why is one properly selected absorbent product insufficient? Possible answers include incorrect sizing, inadequate absorbency, high-output diarrhoea, delayed change intervals, limited staffing, lack of toileting schedule, absence of continence assessment or failure to escalate to specialist care.

Routine layered diapering should be discouraged. It may occasionally be used as a short-term, exceptional measure during transport, procedures or specific clinical circumstances, but only with documentation, time limitation and skin monitoring.

Conclusion

Layered diapering in bedbound adults is an under-researched but clinically relevant practice. Direct evidence is limited, but converging literature on MASD, IAD, skin microclimate, absorbent product use and pressure injury prevention provides a coherent rationale for concern. When used routinely or to extend care intervals, layered diapering may increase occlusion, heat retention, moisture exposure, delayed recognition of saturation and reduced skin inspection.

Implications for Clinical Practice

  • Routine layered diapering should be discouraged in bedbound adults, especially when used to reduce change frequency or prevent linen changes.
  • Adult diapers should be used within an individualised continence and skin-care plan that includes product selection, timely changes, hygiene and skin inspection.
  • Recurrent leakage should trigger reassessment of product size, absorbency, fit, stool consistency, mobility, care interval and staffing workflow.
  • Skin inspection should be mandatory for bedbound adults using absorbent products, particularly in the sacral, gluteal, perineal and groin regions.
  • Layered diapering, when used exceptionally, should be documented with indication, duration and monitoring plan.

Further Research

  • Prospective observational studies should measure the prevalence of layered diapering in acute care, long-term care and home care.
  • Comparative studies should evaluate single versus layered absorbent product use on skin temperature, humidity, pH, saturation detection, comfort and IAD/MASD outcomes.
  • Qualitative studies should explore why nurses and caregivers adopt layered diapering and how workload, staffing and institutional culture influence the practice.

Fecal Contamination in Sacral Pressure Injuries: A Practice-Focused Review

A practice-oriented synthesis of the evidence on fecal contamination as a driver of sacral pressure injury complications, addressing microclimate disruption, dressing failure mechanisms, and the clinical rationale for anatomical barrier protection.

📅 April 2026 ⏱ 12 min read 🔬 SWB Sacral Protection

Introduction

Sacral pressure injuries (SPIs) represent one of the most prevalent and clinically burdensome wound types in hospitalized patients, particularly those in intensive care units (ICUs), long-term care facilities, and post-surgical settings. Among the many factors that complicate their management, fecal contamination remains one of the most underrecognized and systematically underaddressed.

The proximity of the sacral region to the perineum, combined with patient immobility and the high prevalence of fecal incontinence in critically ill populations, creates an almost inevitable risk of microbial contamination of sacral wounds and dressings. Yet clinical protocols and product innovation have historically focused on the wound itself — its depth, moisture balance, and offloading — rather than on interrupting the contamination pathway upstream.

This review synthesizes current evidence on fecal contamination mechanisms, the consequences for wound healing and dressing performance, and the emerging rationale for external anatomical barrier devices as a complementary intervention.

Epidemiology and Context

Pressure injuries affect an estimated 1–3 million patients annually in the United States alone, with sacral and coccygeal locations accounting for approximately 30–40% of all cases. ICU prevalence rates for hospital-acquired pressure injuries (HAPIs) range from 14% to over 40% in some high-acuity cohorts, with sacral injuries disproportionately represented.

Fecal incontinence, present in up to 33% of hospitalized patients and over 50% of ICU patients, is consistently identified as an independent risk factor for SPI development and delayed healing. The mechanisms are multiple and synergistic.

Key Clinical Fact

Fecal incontinence is present in up to 50% of ICU patients and is an independent risk factor for both the development and delayed healing of sacral pressure injuries.

Mechanisms of Fecal Contamination in Sacral Wounds

Microclimate disruption

The wound microclimate — defined by the temperature, humidity, and airflow conditions at the dressing-wound interface — is a critical determinant of healing trajectory. Fecal contamination disrupts this microclimate through multiple pathways:

  • Moisture overload: Liquid stool introduces excessive moisture, overwhelming the absorptive capacity of even high-performance dressings and promoting maceration of the periwound skin.
  • Enzymatic damage: Fecal matter contains proteases, lipases, and other digestive enzymes that degrade both the wound bed and the adhesive components of modern dressings.
  • pH disruption: The alkaline pH of stool disrupts the acidic wound environment that supports fibroblast activity and epithelialization.

Microbial colonization and infection

The human fecal microbiome contains over 1,000 bacterial species. In the context of sacral wound contamination, the most clinically relevant organisms include Escherichia coli, Enterococcus faecalis, Bacteroides fragilis, and Clostridium species. These organisms not only colonize the wound bed but also penetrate dressing layers, compromising sterility and increasing infection risk.

Biofilm formation — a particularly challenging complication — is accelerated by fecal flora, reducing antibiotic efficacy and prolonging the inflammatory phase of wound healing.

Dressing failure

Modern sacral dressings, including foam, hydrocolloid, and silicone varieties, are engineered to maintain a moist healing environment, manage exudate, and protect against shear forces. However, none are designed to withstand direct, repeated fecal contact.

Studies on dressing longevity in incontinent patients consistently demonstrate significantly shortened wear times compared to manufacturer specifications. This accelerates dressing change frequency, increasing nursing workload, patient discomfort, and material costs.

Clinical Consequences and Care Burden

The downstream consequences of fecal contamination in sacral wounds extend well beyond delayed healing:

  • Pressure injury progression: Contaminated wounds are significantly more likely to progress from superficial to deep tissue injury, with associated increases in morbidity and length of stay.
  • Systemic infection risk: In immunocompromised patients, colonized sacral wounds represent a potential source of sepsis, particularly with gram-negative organisms.
  • Nursing workload: Unplanned dressing changes represent one of the largest time expenditures in wound care nursing. In ICU settings, each unplanned change requires repositioning, sterile preparation, and documentation — typically 20–40 minutes of nursing time.
  • Patient experience: Repeated dressing changes are associated with increased pain, anxiety, and skin trauma from adhesive removal, particularly in elderly and frail patients.

Current Mitigation Strategies and Their Limitations

Existing approaches to managing fecal incontinence in the context of sacral wounds include fecal management systems (FMS), barrier creams, and superabsorbent pads. Each has significant limitations:

Fecal management systems (rectal tubes) are effective for fecal diversion but carry risks of rectal mucosal damage, contraindication in patients with recent rectal surgery, and require nursing expertise for insertion and maintenance.

Barrier creams and films protect periwound skin but do not prevent contamination of the wound or dressing surface.

Superabsorbent pads manage moisture but maintain close contact between fecal matter and the wound region, allowing enzyme and microbial penetration over time.

"There is a clear clinical gap between existing containment approaches and true wound-level protection in incontinent patients with sacral pressure injuries. Anatomical barrier devices represent a promising direction for addressing this gap."

The Rationale for Anatomical Barrier Devices

An ideal adjunct intervention would intercept fecal material before it reaches the sacral wound or dressing, without requiring invasive placement, patient cooperation, or significant nursing time for application.

External anatomical barrier devices — positioned to conform to the gluteal and sacral anatomy — offer a non-invasive approach to creating this protective separation. By redirecting fecal flow away from the wound zone, such devices can potentially:

  • Extend dressing wear time and reduce unplanned changes
  • Preserve wound microclimate integrity
  • Reduce microbial load at the wound surface
  • Decrease nursing time associated with contamination management
  • Improve patient comfort during wound care episodes

Conclusion

Fecal contamination represents a mechanistically well-understood, clinically significant, and largely underaddressed driver of sacral pressure injury complications. Current management strategies address its consequences — contaminated wounds, failed dressings, and overburdened nursing teams — rather than interrupting the contamination pathway itself.

A non-invasive anatomical barrier approach offers a promising and pragmatic solution that is compatible with existing wound care protocols and applicable across care settings. Further clinical validation, including comparative effectiveness studies and health economic analyses, is warranted to establish the evidence base for routine adoption.

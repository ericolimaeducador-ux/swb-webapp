Biofilm and Wound Hygiene in Hard-to-Heal Wounds: A Practice-Focused Review

A practice-focused review examining how biofilm recognition changes wound care from passive dressing replacement to structured wound hygiene, repeated wound-bed preparation and biofilm-informed clinical reassessment.

May 2026 Scientific manuscript Journal of Wound Management / Cambridge-style preparation

Abstract

Background: Biofilm is increasingly recognised as a major local barrier to healing in hard-to-heal wounds. However, in routine practice it is often treated as a laboratory concept rather than as a factor that should change daily wound-bed preparation.

Hypothesis/Aim: This review aimed to examine how recognition of biofilm should modify wound care practice, shifting clinicians from passive dressing replacement toward structured wound hygiene, including cleansing, debridement, wound-edge management and biofilm-informed dressing selection.

Methods: A practice-focused narrative review was conducted. Sources included international consensus documents, clinical guidelines, systematic reviews, narrative reviews and clinically relevant papers addressing wound biofilm, wound infection, hard-to-heal wounds, wound hygiene, debridement, wound-bed preparation and antimicrobial stewardship.

Results/Findings: Biofilm is highly prevalent in chronic wounds and is associated with delayed healing, persistent inflammation, antimicrobial tolerance and recurrent local wound deterioration. Because bedside confirmation is rarely available, biofilm should be clinically suspected in hard-to-heal wounds that fail to progress despite appropriate aetiological management.

Conclusions: Biofilm should be understood as a practice-changing concept. In hard-to-heal wounds, clinicians should move beyond passive dressing replacement and adopt structured, repeated wound hygiene as part of routine care.

Implications for clinical practice: Wound assessment should incorporate biofilm suspicion, active cleansing, maintenance debridement, wound-edge management, appropriate antimicrobial use and objective reassessment of healing trajectory.

Introduction

Hard-to-heal wounds remain a persistent clinical, economic and organisational challenge across acute care, long-term care, outpatient services and home care. Venous leg ulcers, diabetic foot ulcers, pressure injuries, arterial ulcers and complex surgical wounds may fail to progress despite apparently appropriate dressing selection. In practice, this often generates a cycle of repeated dressing changes without sufficient reassessment of the wound bed, wound edge, bioburden, host factors and underlying aetiology.

Biofilm has become central to contemporary explanations of wound chronicity. Malone et al. estimated that biofilm was present in 78.2% of chronic wounds, supporting the view that biofilm is not an occasional phenomenon but a frequent feature of human chronic non-healing wounds [1]. Classic and contemporary wound biofilm literature describes biofilms as structured microbial communities embedded in an extracellular polymeric matrix, with increased tolerance to antimicrobials, host defences and environmental stress compared with planktonic microorganisms [2-4].

The clinical relevance of biofilm is not limited to microbiology. Biofilm may help explain why some wounds remain locked in a chronic inflammatory state, with recurrent slough, persistent exudate, friable granulation tissue, odour, delayed epithelialisation and failure to respond to standard care. However, biofilm is difficult to diagnose at the bedside. Routine swab culture does not reliably identify biofilm structure or spatial distribution. Advanced microscopy and molecular methods are rarely available in everyday wound care.

The International Wound Infection Institute 2022 consensus document emphasises updated concepts related to wound infection, biofilm, wound cleansing, debridement and antimicrobial stewardship [5]. In parallel, the Wound Hygiene consensus reframes care of hard-to-heal wounds as an active, repeated intervention directed at disrupting biofilm and preparing the wound bed through four steps: cleanse, debride, refashion wound edges and dress [6].

Aim

The aim of this review was to examine how recognition of biofilm modifies wound care practice in hard-to-heal wounds, with specific focus on wound hygiene, cleansing, debridement, wound-edge management, dressing selection and antimicrobial stewardship.

Added value of this review

This review reframes biofilm as an operational clinical concept, integrates biofilm science with daily wound hygiene practices and proposes a practical biofilm-to-practice framework to support assessment, intervention and reassessment in hard-to-heal wounds.

Methods

Design

A practice-focused narrative review was conducted because the topic requires integration of mechanistic biofilm science, clinical consensus documents, wound infection guidance, wound-bed preparation principles and practical wound hygiene strategies.

Search strategy

A structured search strategy was developed around six concepts: wound biofilm, chronic wounds, hard-to-heal wounds, wound infection, wound hygiene and wound-bed preparation. Searches were planned and conducted across PubMed/MEDLINE, Google Scholar and key wound-care publication platforms.

Search terms included “biofilm”, “wound biofilm”, “chronic wound biofilm”, “hard-to-heal wounds”, “wound hygiene”, “wound bed preparation”, “debridement”, “maintenance debridement”, “wound infection”, “local wound infection”, “antimicrobial stewardship”, “wound cleansing” and “wound edge management”.

Eligibility and appraisal

Eligible sources addressed biofilm in chronic or hard-to-heal wounds, wound infection, wound hygiene, wound cleansing, debridement, wound-bed preparation, wound-edge management, antimicrobial stewardship or clinical management of hard-to-heal wounds. Higher priority was given to international consensus documents, systematic reviews and clinically relevant reviews.

Ethical considerations

This review did not involve human participants, animal research, patient identifiers or primary data collection. Ethical approval and informed consent were not required.

Results

1. Biofilm is common in hard-to-heal wounds but difficult to confirm clinically

Biofilm is frequently identified in chronic wounds, but routine clinical confirmation remains difficult. Malone et al. reported a pooled biofilm prevalence of 78.2% in chronic wounds, although the included studies were limited by small sample sizes and heterogeneity [1]. Slough, exudate and delayed healing may suggest biofilm but are not specific. The clinical challenge is therefore probabilistic: wound professionals must decide when biofilm is sufficiently likely to justify active anti-biofilm wound-bed preparation.

2. Biofilm tolerance explains why passive dressing changes often fail

Biofilm-associated microorganisms are more tolerant to antimicrobials and host responses than planktonic organisms [4]. If microorganisms are organised in biofilm structures, simply applying a topical antimicrobial dressing without disrupting the wound surface may be insufficient. Effective management requires physical disruption, removal of devitalised tissue, control of exudate, correction of underlying aetiology and reduction of biofilm reformation.

3. Biofilm should be clinically suspected in stalled wounds

Biofilm should be considered in wounds that fail to progress despite appropriate management of aetiology, perfusion, pressure, oedema, nutrition and systemic factors. Clinical features that may support suspicion include delayed healing, recurrent slough, persistent or increasing exudate, friable granulation tissue, malodour, subtle local inflammation, pocketing, undermining, wound breakdown after apparent improvement and repeated failure of advanced dressings.

4. Wound Hygiene translates biofilm science into practice

Wound Hygiene consists of four steps: cleansing, debridement, refashioning the wound edges and dressing the wound [6]. Cleansing is not limited to rinsing the wound. It includes active cleansing of the wound bed and periwound skin. Debridement removes devitalised tissue and disrupts biofilm-containing material. Refashioning the wound edge addresses non-advancing, rolled or thickened edges that may interfere with epithelial migration.

5. Debridement is central to biofilm management

Debridement is one of the most important interventions for biofilm disruption. It may be sharp, conservative sharp, surgical, mechanical, autolytic, enzymatic, biological or combined, depending on patient condition, clinician competency, wound type, pain, perfusion, bleeding risk and setting [8,9]. Biofilm can reform after disruption; therefore, hard-to-heal wounds may require repeated or maintenance debridement.

6. Wound-edge management is often neglected

The wound edge is frequently underemphasised in routine care. A non-advancing, rolled, undermined or hyperkeratotic edge can represent a barrier to epithelial migration. From a biofilm-informed perspective, the wound edge should not be treated as a passive boundary but as part of the healing interface.

7. Antimicrobial dressings require indication, duration and reassessment

Biofilm recognition should not lead to indiscriminate antimicrobial use. Antimicrobial dressings may be appropriate in wounds with suspected or confirmed local infection, high bioburden or recurrent deterioration, but they should be used as part of a broader wound-bed preparation strategy [5,10].

8. Biofilm care requires reassessment of the whole wound system

Biofilm is rarely the only reason a wound fails to heal. Venous hypertension, oedema, arterial disease, pressure, diabetes, neuropathy, malnutrition, immunosuppression, pain, poor adherence, inappropriate footwear, inadequate compression and insufficient offloading may all sustain chronicity. Biofilm-informed care must therefore be integrated with holistic wound assessment.

Discussion

This review supports a clear clinical position: biofilm should be treated as a practice-changing concept in hard-to-heal wound care. The importance of biofilm lies not only in its microbiological structure, but in the practical response it requires. A wound suspected of biofilm should not receive repeated passive dressing changes without active cleansing, debridement, wound-edge assessment and objective reassessment.

One of the most important clinical challenges is that biofilm cannot usually be confirmed in routine care. A defensible approach is clinical probability: biofilm should be suspected when a wound remains hard to heal despite appropriate management and when local signs suggest persistent inflammation or recurrent surface contamination.

Wound care is often product-centred. Biofilm reframes the role of dressings. Dressings are important, but they are not the primary intervention when the wound bed is biologically unprepared. The dressing should consolidate the effect of wound-bed preparation, manage exudate, maintain an appropriate healing environment and, where indicated, support antimicrobial objectives.

The implementation challenge is practical. Wound Hygiene must be taught as a routine clinical behaviour, not as an expert-only procedure. Nurses and wound professionals need training in active cleansing, safe debridement within scope, wound-edge recognition, pain control, periwound protection, documentation and reassessment.

Biofilm-to-practice framework
  1. Suspect biofilm in hard-to-heal wounds with stalled healing, chronic inflammation, recurrent slough, persistent exudate or repeated dressing failure.
  2. Reassess aetiology, perfusion, pressure, oedema, offloading, nutrition, pain, systemic disease and adherence.
  3. Disrupt surface barriers through active cleansing and appropriate debridement.
  4. Refashion non-advancing, rolled, callused, undermined or macerated wound edges.
  5. Dress according to wound goals: exudate control, periwound protection, antimicrobial objective where indicated and patient comfort.
  6. Re-evaluate healing trajectory objectively within a defined time frame and step up or step down care accordingly.

Conclusion

Biofilm is not only a laboratory concept. In hard-to-heal wounds, it should change how clinicians assess, clean, debride, dress and reassess the wound. Because bedside confirmation is rarely available, biofilm should be approached as a clinical probability in wounds that fail to progress despite appropriate care. The correct response is structured wound hygiene: cleanse, debride, manage wound edges, dress with a clear objective and reassess healing trajectory.

Implications for Clinical Practice

  • Biofilm should be considered in hard-to-heal wounds that fail to progress despite appropriate management of aetiology and patient factors.
  • Wound care should move beyond passive dressing replacement toward structured wound hygiene at each relevant dressing change.
  • Cleansing should include the wound bed and periwound skin, with the objective of removing debris, exudate and surface contaminants.
  • Debridement should be considered as a repeated or maintenance strategy when slough, devitalised tissue or delayed tissue repair persists.
  • Wound edges should be assessed systematically and managed when rolled, callused, undermined, thickened or non-advancing.
  • Antimicrobial dressings should be used with clear indication, defined goals, reassessment and discontinuation or step-down when appropriate.

Further Research

  • Prospective studies should evaluate whether structured Wound Hygiene improves healing outcomes compared with standard dressing-focused care.
  • Research should define practical bedside indicators that improve clinical suspicion of biofilm without overdiagnosis.
  • Comparative studies are needed to assess different cleansing and debridement strategies for suspected biofilm in specific wound types.
  • Implementation studies should examine how nurses and wound-care teams adopt Wound Hygiene in acute care, outpatient clinics, long-term care and home care.
